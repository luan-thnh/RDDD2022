How Much Does Road-Damage Detection Degrade
Across Acquisition Domains?
A Scene-Aware Bidirectional Evaluation on
RDD2022
Lê Thành Luân∗, Phạm Bá Anh Tuấn†
University of Science and Technology – The University of Danang, Da Nang, Vietnam
∗102250416@hv.dut.udn.vn; †102250421@hv.dut.udn.vn
Abstract—Road-damage detectors are often evaluated on im-
agery resembling their training data, yet deployment across
camera platforms can reduce accuracy. This study quantifies the
magnitude, asymmetry, and class dependence of cross-domain
transfer between the China_Drone and China_MotorBike subsets
of RDD2022. We use a scene-aware split and compare target-
domain-trained and other-domain-trained detectors on identical
held-out target images. The locked subset contains 4,378 images,
including 658 test images, and four damage classes. COCO-
pretrained YOLO11n, YOLOv8n, and YOLO26n are indepen-
dently fine-tuned on each domain with three matched seeds and
150 epochs per run (18 runs). Mean fixed-target mAP@50:95
gaps range from 14.86 to 16.91 percentage points on Drone Test
and from 41.24 to 42.94 points on MotorBike Test. For YOLO26n,
the respective gaps are 16.91 ± 0.80 and 42.27 ± 1.28 points,
where uncertainty is sample standard deviation over three seeds.
All 18 seed-specific comparisons favor target-domain training.
On MotorBike Test, D20 relative class-AP declines range from
96.14% to 98.30% against the target-trained baselines. YOLO26n
improves mean in-domain mAP over YOLOv8n by 3.08 and 3.50
points on Drone and MotorBike, respectively, while substantial
gaps persist. The study contributes a reproducible bidirectional
protocol and a three-variant replication of domain-associated
asymmetry and class-specific vulnerability. Acquisition geometry,
training size, and other collection differences remain jointly
varying factors.

I. Introduction
Automated inspection of pavement distress is attractive be-
cause manual surveys are costly and difficult to repeat at scale.
Early road-damage detection studies established vehicle-image
benchmarks [1], while RDD2022 provided a broader collection
with four common damage categories [2]. A detector fine-
tuned on overhead images, however, may encounter very
different object scales and road context when deployed with
a camera mounted near road level. Whether such a detector
retains its accuracy must be established empirically.
There is a common ambiguity in cross-domain evaluation.
Comparing one model on two different test sets combines
model transfer with differences in the intrinsic difficulty of
those datasets. Instead, we compare a detector trained on the
test domain against a detector trained on the other domain,
using the same held-out target images. We evaluate both
transfer directions. This isolates test-set composition within
a comparison, although it cannot isolate the causes of the
training-domain difference.
We address four research questions: RQ1: How large and
asymmetric are the fixed-target performance gaps? RQ2: Are
gap directions and magnitudes consistent across random seeds
and the three tested YOLO variants? RQ3: How does transfer
vary among D00, D10, D20, and D40? RQ4: Does the addition
of YOLO26n improve absolute accuracy while reducing or
preserving domain-gap magnitude? Our contributions are:
(1) a reproducible bidirectional evaluation with scene-aware
partitions and fixed target test sets; (2) seed-resolved evidence
of target asymmetry and class-specific vulnerability; and (3)
replication across YOLO11n, YOLOv8n, and YOLO26n under
matched explicit fine-tuning settings. The study provides a
descriptive evaluation of source-domain choice rather than
a new detector or an isolated causal estimate of viewpoint
effects.
II. Related Work
Consensus: domain shift degrades detection. Multiple
studies agree that learned predictors trained on one visual
distribution suffer performance loss when deployed under
different acquisition conditions. The domain-generalization
literature cautions that success on source-like data may not
carry to a shifted target distribution [3]; benchmarks such as
WILDS [4] illustrate that preserving meaningful dataset bound-
aries is essential for valid performance claims. Road-damage
detection follows this pattern: Maeda et al. [1] introduced
smartphone-image detection and classification, and Arya et al.
[2] documented the multinational RDD2022 dataset’s acquisi-
tion diversity, providing a setting in which camera-platform
transfer can be examined.
Trade-off: adaptation vs. controlled measurement. One
line of work develops architectures or adaptation strategies
to improve cross-domain robustness. For example, YOLO-
RD [5] proposes detector enhancements for pavement distress.

Such enhancement-oriented studies typically evaluate on a par-
ticular subset with architecture-specific tuning, making direct
numerical comparison across papers difficult when dataset
partitions, model sizes, label filtering, and validation protocols
are not aligned. An alternative approach—adopted here—
holds each detector’s explicit fine-tuning settings fixed while
varying the source acquisition domain, prioritizing a clear
measurement of domain-associated performance differences.
YOLO family evolution. YOLOv8 [6], YOLO11 [7], and
YOLO26 [8] provide related compact detector variants with
different pretrained checkpoints and model-specific implemen-
tations. Repeating the same fixed-target protocol across them
tests whether the observed gap is confined to one variant.
Matched explicit fine-tuning settings improve comparability,
while architecture, pretraining, and implementation differences
remain coupled.
Gap: bidirectional asymmetry and class-level vulnerabil-
ity. Comparing one model on two different test sets conflates
transfer behavior with intrinsic test-set difficulty. A fixed-
target comparison is needed to quantify the advantage of target-
domain training on each acquisition domain and to determine
how that contrast varies across D00, D10, D20, and D40.
The question addressed here is how large these two target-
specific gaps are, whether their direction recurs across seeds
and models, and which classes depart from the aggregate
pattern.

An earlier image/XML audit checked image–annotation
correspondence, valid bounding boxes, class mapping, and
candidate duplicate scenes. The review identified 735 high-
priority visual-similarity pairs: 629 were marked same-scene,

groups, uncertain pairs, and flagged medium/low-priority can-

under a conservative protection rule. This policy controls
identified cross-partition links; it does not prove that all

V. Results
A. RQ1: Bidirectional Fixed-Target Gaps
Table III contains the central result. All three models favor
training on the evaluation domain, but the advantage is larger
on MotorBike Test. For YOLO11n, the three-seed mean gap
is 15.13 ± 3.85 pp on Drone Test versus 42.94 ± 0.52 pp
on MotorBike Test. YOLOv8n shows corresponding gaps of
14.86±1.25 and 41.24±1.04 pp. YOLO26n adds correspond-
ing gaps of 16.91 ± 0.80 and 42.27 ± 1.28 pp. This supports
a strong, asymmetric association between training acquisition
domain and target performance; it is not a causal estimate of
drone height or camera angle.
B. Convergence and Training Stability
Figure 1 presents the original representative training and
validation trajectories for YOLO11n trained on Drone across
three random seeds. Training loss declined steadily, while
validation loss fluctuated before stabilizing. Maximum logged
validation mAP@50:95 occurred at epochs 110, 92, and 111
for seeds 13, 37, and 71, respectively. Across the full ex-
periment, all 18 runs completed 150 epochs with patience
set to zero; the maximum logged validation-mAP epochs
ranged from 63 to 133. The histories support completed,
stable training under the stated budget. A pre-final-epoch
validation peak does not establish global convergence or an
optimal schedule, and maximum logged mAP alone does not
independently verify the fitness rule used to save best.pt.
C. RQ2: Seed Stability and Architecture Replication
For YOLO11n, paired gaps on Drone Test are 16.27, 10.84,
and 18.29 pp for seeds 13, 37, and 71, respectively. On Mo-
torBike Test they are 43.49, 42.47, and 42.85 pp. YOLOv8n
reproduces the positive direction in all three seeds. Its Drone-
target gaps are 15.03, 13.52, and 16.01 pp; its MotorBike-
target gaps are 40.39, 42.40, and 40.93 pp. YOLO26n
adds Drone-target gaps of 16.91, 16.10, and 17.71 pp and
MotorBike-target gaps of 43.14, 42.87, and 40.80 pp. The
gap direction is replicated across all three variants and all 18
model–target–seed comparisons, while the seed-to-seed spread
differs, especially on Drone Test. The three reused seed values
and three variants from one model family support within-
family replication; architectural changes remain coupled with
pretraining and model-specific implementation differences.
D. RQ3: Class-Wise Heterogeneity
Figure 3 compares the class-specific target-trained advan-
tage. D00 and D10 show positive fixed-target differences
on both targets. D20 shows a particularly large contrast on
MotorBike Test, where all three variants reach substantially
higher AP after training on MotorBike than after training on
Drone. We retain D40 in all four-class primary metrics; we do
not repair labels or change the split to improve observed AP.
D40 is harder to interpret on Drone Test: only 17 annotated
pothole boxes occur on nine images, many associated with
one scene group, so AP changes can be dominated by a small
number of detections.
Table VII makes the class-specific variation explicit for all
three models. For MotorBike Test, D20 shows large target-
trained advantages in each model (57.72, 53.21, and 56.45 pp
for YOLO11n, YOLOv8n, and YOLO26n), whereas D40
on Drone Test shows negative mean fixed-target advantages
(−6.61, −9.74, and −1.95 pp, respectively) with sparse sup-
port. This is an observational difference, not an architecture-
level explanation of pothole recognition. The archived outputs
do not provide an independent per-image false-positive/false-
negative audit, so we do not attribute individual class failures
to a particular visual mechanism.
E. RQ4: Multi-Architecture Comparison
Figure 4 compares all three variants on each fixed target.
YOLO26n achieves the highest mean in-domain mAP@50:95:
24.49% on Drone Test and 54.75% on MotorBike Test. These
exceed YOLOv8n by 3.08 and 3.50 pp, corresponding to
relative gains of 14.38% and 6.83%. Its fixed-target gaps
nevertheless remain 16.91 and 42.27 pp. Dividing each gap
by the mean target-trained score on that same target gives
relative declines of 69.04% and 77.20%. Thus, the additional
model strengthens the original finding: higher in-domain mean
accuracy does not eliminate the cross-domain deficit.

VI. Discussion
The same-target design removes a major interpretive prob-
lem of subtracting scores from two different test sets. It
does not equalize the sizes or composition of Drone and
MotorBike training data (1,680 versus 1,383 images), nor their
acquisition conditions. It is therefore more precise to describe
the result as a domain-associated source-training gap than an
experimentally isolated viewpoint effect. A substantial positive
gap appears across all three compact YOLO variants and all
seed-specific comparisons, especially when MotorBike is the
target; this is the empirical finding supported by the archive.
Why does performance degrade? Three factors provide
hypotheses for interpreting the observed domain gap. First,
scale mismatch: overhead and near-ground imagery can
present defects at different pixel scales and perspectives,
potentially changing the visual information available to a
detector. Object-scale distributions and paired views were not
measured here, so a scale mechanism remains to be tested.
Second, context distribution shift: different roadway framing
and surrounding backgrounds may alter source-domain cues.
The fixed-target contrast shows that source choice matters
but does not distinguish context from illumination, road-
surface differences, or annotation density. Third, class-specific
fragility: on MotorBike Test, D20 AP falls from 58.72% to
1.00% for YOLO11n, from 55.35% to 2.14% for YOLOv8n,
and from 57.99% to 1.54% for YOLO26n when target-domain
training is replaced by Drone training. Relative to the target-
trained class means, these declines are 98.30%, 96.14%, and
97.34%; D00 declines on the same target are smaller at
56.57%, 56.37%, and 51.88%. Fine-texture and scale sensitiv-
ity are plausible explanations for this class contrast, requiring
controlled or per-image analysis to establish their roles.
Comparison with prior work. Our findings support
the motivation for distribution-aware evaluation in domain-
generalization research and WILDS [3], [4], while focusing on
two road-acquisition domains and their class-specific contrasts.
The fixed-target protocol complements detector-enhancement
work such as YOLO-RD [5]; we do not numerically rank
studies with incompatible partitions and label policies. COCO
[9] supplies the pretraining context rather than evidence of
adaptation on these road datasets. The MotorBike-target ad-
vantage exceeds the Drone-target advantage by 27.80, 26.38,
and 25.36 pp for YOLO11n, YOLOv8n, and YOLO26n,
respectively. This quantifies the difference between two target-
specific gaps without establishing that either acquisition plat-
form intrinsically produces more transferable features.
A. Limitations
Several design choices bound the interpretation. Training
sizes differ (1,680 versus 1,383 images), and acquisition
domain can also vary with location, roadway condition, illu-
mination, and annotation density. No balancing or ablation
study separates these factors. Three seed values and three
compact variants from one YOLO family support replication
on the fixed datasets, not precise population-level inference
or architecture-independent generalization. Both domains are
China subsets, so transfer to other countries and devices
remains unmeasured. D40 has sparse Drone support, and
filtered classes outside D00–D40 are not ignore regions. These
constraints motivate targeted controls rather than treating the
observed gaps as isolated viewpoint effects.
B. Threats to Validity
The scene-review and conservative grouping address iden-
tified similarity candidates but cannot guarantee that all latent
scene overlap was found. Some MotorBike images were
encountered during exploratory analysis before split final-
ization, limiting an entirely untouched-holdout claim. Clean-
only evaluation retains the gap direction but changes the
image population. The present analysis checks and recomputes
archived metric summaries; it does not independently rerun

VII. Conclusion
On the scene-reviewed RDD2022 v4.1 Drone/MotorBike
split, 18 fine-tuning runs across YOLO11n, YOLOv8n, and
YOLO26n show consistent in-domain advantages under fixed-
target evaluation. Mean gaps range from 14.86 to 16.91 pp
on Drone Test and from 41.24 to 42.94 pp on MotorBike
Test, with distinct class-level behavior. Key quantitative
findings: (1) all 18 full-test seed comparisons and their clean-
only counterparts favor target-domain training; (2) MotorBike-
target D20 relative AP declines range from 96.14% to 98.30%
against the target-trained baselines, while sparse Drone-target
D40 shows negative mean class gaps; and (3) MotorBike-
target advantages exceed Drone-target advantages by 25.36–
27.80 pp across the three variants. YOLO26n raises in-domain
mean accuracy while preserving the substantial cross-domain
gap.
Practical
implications:
Practitioners
deploying
road-
damage detectors across camera platforms should budget for
substantial mAP degradation, validate performance on the
intended target platform, and prioritize retraining or domain
adaptation when acquisition geometry changes. Class-level

validation is important because aggregate mAP can hide large
D20 losses and sparse-class reversals. Future work should
validate per-image predictions independently, quantify scene-
level uncertainty, test additional acquisition domains (India,
Czech, Japan subsets) under an unchanged audit protocol,
and explore multi-domain training strategies to improve cross-
platform robustness.
